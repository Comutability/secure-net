## 1. Problem

A desk review of Secure Net against Signal and Matrix (2026-11-14) found the session design in
Signal's class and ahead of Matrix's deployed encryption on quantum resistance, with these gaps:

1. The handshake transcript bound prekeys by id only, and keys for missing messages never
   expired.
2. Every message carried about 2.3 KB of ML-KEM material.
3. Sites saw who wrote to whom, and could link the messages of a conversation.
4. Groups used sender keys: no recovery after a compromise until every member made a new key.
5. Every direct message was signed, so a recipient could prove what they were sent.

The largest gap, independent review and a formal model, is not something a change to the
protocol can close and is not addressed here.

## 2. Proposed change

- **CVP-016 version 2.** Prekey public keys in the transcript; the initiator's identity
  encrypted in the first message; encrypted headers; ML-KEM keys and ciphertexts sent in pieces
  inside headers, with one new secret mixed into the root key per completed exchange; keys for
  missing messages deleted after 7 days.
- **CVP-019 version 2.** Direct envelopes with no session tag; unsigned direct activities;
  sealed sending with delivery tokens; group envelopes.
- **CVP-022.** Groups on MLS (RFC 9420) with the X-Wing and ML-DSA-87 suite, credentials signed
  by Secure Net devices, and a single committer per group.

## 3. Alternatives considered

- **ML-KEM at every ratchet step, as version 1.** Fastest recovery against a quantum attacker,
  but 3.1 KB on every message. Signal made the same trade when it added its sparse post-quantum
  ratchet.
- **Erasure-coded pieces, as Signal's.** More efficient when messages are lost. Sending pieces
  in turn is simpler to specify and check, and encrypted headers stop an attacker choosing which
  piece to drop. Worth revisiting.
- **A group protocol of our own with a key tree.** It would have no public analysis. MLS has.
- **Any member may commit.** Needs one order of commits, which needs a server to choose it or a
  rule for resolving forks. A single committer is the simplest rule that is safe; it costs
  availability when the committer is away.
- **Keeping direct messages signed.** Lets recipients prove what they received (useful for
  reports), at the cost of everyone's deniability. Left as a sender's choice.

## 4. Compatibility

Additive. A device announces version 2 in its signed prekey; sites announce `cvp.sessions: 2`,
`cvp.delivery: 2` and `cvp.groups: 1`. Version 1 sessions keep working and are deprecated.
Existing version 1 sessions are not upgraded in place: a new session is started. Groups need an
MLS library (`ts-mls`), which is an optional dependency; nothing else needs it.

## 5. Security and privacy

- None of this has had human cryptographic review. `ts-mls` states it has had no formal audit.
- Version 2 recovers from a compromise against a quantum attacker more slowly than version 1
  (about every 22 messages of back-and-forth, not every reply).
- Sealed sending hides the sender from both sites but not the sender's network address, and a
  token holder can send unwanted messages until the token is replaced.
- Unsigned direct messages can't be proved to a moderator.
- A group stops changing while its committer is offline.
- MLS signs with ML-DSA-87 only; credentials and posts keep hybrid signatures.
